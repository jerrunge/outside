Field school

rig it, knot it, fish it

Three rigs, easiest first

Hopper-dropper (start here)

Tie a buoyant dry (foam hopper or Elk Hair Caddis) to your tippet with an improved clinch knot. Then tie 18–24″ of tippet to the BEND of that hook and add a Pheasant Tail nymph. The dry floats and doubles as your strike indicator; the nymph does the catching. Highest-odds beginner rig.

Single dry

During a visible hatch or rising fish, fish just the dry (Parachute Adams / caddis). Watch it drift; set gently when a fish eats it.

Nymph under indicator

When nothing's rising: indicator up the leader about 1.5× the water's depth, a weighted nymph plus one split shot, dead-drifted through the deeper runs.

The only three knots you need

  • Loop-to-loop: fly-line loop ↔ leader loop, no knot to tie.
  • Leader → tippet: double surgeon's knot (or a blood knot).
  • Tippet → fly: improved clinch knot. Wet it, seat it slowly.

How to actually fish it

  1. Practice first. The evening before, string a rod and cast on the lawn for twenty minutes. Ten-to-two, pause on the backcast to let the rod load, keep it short — 20–30 ft is plenty.
  2. Read the water. Trout hold where fast meets slow: seams beside the current, behind boulders, the heads and tails of pools, foam lines ("foam is home"), and shaded undercut banks.
  3. Approach like a heron. The water is low and gin-clear. Come from downstream, stay low, keep your shadow and feet quiet. The first cast to a spot is the best cast.
  4. Get a drag-free drift. Cast up and across; let the flies drift at exactly the current's speed. Flip a mend (a small upstream flick of line) so the current doesn't drag the fly. The dead drift is the whole game.
  5. Set and land. On a dry, lift the rod gently when the fish eats; on the dropper/indicator, set at any pause. Rod tip up, let it run, net it quickly in the rubber mesh.
  6. Release it right (required over 12″): barbless hook, wet hands, fish stays in the water, minimal air, face it upstream until it kicks off.

Before you buy · fine print

Pinch every barb. It's the law on the Tuolumne system and it makes unhooking a fish — or your own ear — painless. Free; just squeeze each hook flat with your forceps.
License. Anyone 16+ needs a CA sport-fishing license. Resident annual is $64.54 (covers every other Get Out trip); a 2-day is $32.40. Buy at wildlife.ca.gov — the digital license on the CDFW app counts as proof.
